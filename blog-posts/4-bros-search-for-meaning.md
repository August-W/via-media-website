The Newark air was frozen in silence. Several blocks from my dormitory, a small, secluded park provided brief respite from concrete and industry. This was the site of my existential crisis. I walked slowly, as though through deep Russian snow, on my spontaneous sunset stroll. Crime and Punishment was fermenting, permeating deep into my skull. My very vision seemed clearer as I looked afresh at my surroundings, my brick Saint Petersburg. I have been living like Raskolnikov. The realization was convicting. It was this Russian nesting doll of successively deeper ruminations that marked the start of my spiritual journey: that right and wrong were real and objective, and that, maybe in some sense, so too was God. This is the story of how a one-time frat bro came to Christ.

From the day of my Roman Catholic baptism, I was raised without any religious faith. My mother claimed to be an atheist, though she prayed in times of need, and my father took up an interest in Hinduism by way of George Harrison. They supported me through my higher education, where I promptly sought out the clichéd college experience of leftist politics and beer-fueled depravity. Despite my childish penchant for such formulaic forms of transgression, my parents did impart to me a love of learning and a genuine curiosity about all manner of ideas. So I eventually read beyond Lenin and Kropotkin, slowly moderating, until ultimately realizing that all my beliefs were utterly backward. 

With my entire worldview collapsing, along with my ability to graduate, I concluded that I needed a rule of life. I broke bro-code and abandoned the debauched basement parties that so battered my GPA.  I meditated, studied virtue ethics and the Stoics, and attempted to piece it all together into something coherent. Crime and Punishment shook me, and Jordan Peterson’s Genesis lectures stirred me. Stuck in my head, like the melody of a balalaika, was the notion that I was indefensibly negligent in dismissing this whole “Christianity thing.” 

In time, I nurtured an appreciation for Christianity as the foundation of western civilization. I began to entertain thoughts about making Roman Catholicism my rule of life. I believed it to be good and beautiful, though I was not yet ready to accept the truth of Christ. Nevertheless, an inkling remained in the back of my mind: that with a religious lifestyle, a genuine faith may follow. 

For years, I casually considered Roman Catholicism, consuming books and podcasts, but procrastinating on the next step. My inner bro-skolnikov wanted to hold onto some sins for a little while longer. This was when I met my girlfriend – my first love and the catalyst for my spiritual progress… a walking Dostoevsky novel. I cannot overstate how much it affected me to encounter someone with such a genuinely caring heart. Meeting a woman so good and so beautiful forced me to confront my own sin and ugliness, and take action. I was no longer to be a mere hearer of the Word but a doer.

What followed was a period of visiting various churches, researching apologetics, and praying. My faith developed gradually, as I grew convinced of Christ’s resurrection and cultivated a personal relationship to Him through prayer. I also began to think that I ought not arbitrarily default to Roman Catholicism out of familiarity and aesthetic preference. Investigating the other denominations, Anglicanism caught my interest. The influence of Burkean conservatism on my perspective naturally drew me to the conservative approach taken by Edmund Burke’s great influence, Richard Hooker, in the reformation. A reformed catholicism with a nuanced posture toward tradition rang true to me. And so I visited Anglican Church of the Holy Spirit. 

My first Sunday at Anglican Church of the Holy Spirit felt in some ways familiar, with one particularly remarkable distinction: everyone sang the hymns. I was moved almost to tears when I experienced “Holy, Holy, Holy” for the first time. Father Carl’s services were both reverent and joyful, his homilies delivered with humor that did not detract from their depth. I visited again, and then again.

I suddenly found that my daily life had rather diverged from a year prior, let alone from my rampaging-cossack-college-bro days. I was praying, reading scripture, spending much of my free time studying theology and Christian history, and even attending church. The Book of Common Prayer was foundational to the rule of life for which I so thirsted – but this was no longer about mere psychological self-improvement. I took a day to ensconce myself in prayer – it was finally time to make this official. Kneeling at my prayer corner in the flickering candlelight, beneath the painted faces of the icons of Saint John Chrysostom and Saint Athanasius, I slowly inhaled the scent of burning beeswax and simply accepted the truth of my situation. I was giving myself to Christ. 

Far from bringing my hunger to a muted end, that only amplified my appetite for theological inquiry. Sacramental theology, the Church Fathers, chants and hymnody… all of it was captivating. Deacon Shane’s catechesis likewise fed my interest with profoundly engaging conversations – I left each week feeling inspired. From my father sharing his enthusiasm for eastern mysticism, to my own preoccupation with political philosophy in college, to my circuitous path to faith, all of these experiences effectively shaped me into someone who would be eager to pursue a seminary education. I receive an incredible sense of purpose from serving my church, and I am certain that these studies will enable me to further serve in some future lay ministry. And, though ordination still seems unimaginable, the thought has occurred to me that it would be deeply rewarding to catechize someone else someday, as Deacon Shane catechized me. I am immensely grateful to him, and to all the other voices that helped guide me out of the wilderness: my girlfriend, Father Carl, Jordan Peterson, my parents, Fyodor Dostoevsky, and He who revealed Himself to me through them.
